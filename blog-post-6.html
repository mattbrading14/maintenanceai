A preventive maintenance calendar that records when the work actually happened

A useful small-building maintenance calendar connects each recurring task to an asset, an owner, a due date, and a source for its interval. Closeout then records the actual service date, evidence, and the basis for the next due date. Those dates may fall in different months.

Build the calendar from your building’s equipment

  1. List the assets and locations. Start with the equipment and common areas you actually manage: ventilation, heating and cooling, plumbing, lighting, and the building exterior. Keep required professional inspections in the list with their own source and responsible provider.
  2. Define the task and its basis. Use the equipment manual, qualified service provider’s written plan, and applicable inspection requirements to establish the work and interval. Record the source so another manager can understand the schedule.
  3. Assign responsibility and a scheduled due date. A reminder needs a named person to arrange access, confirm the visit, and follow up. Separate the appointment from the deadline; booking a visit does not complete the task.
  4. Make a monthly view. Filter by scheduled due to see what is coming up. Carry overdue work forward with its original due date and a follow-up action. Include a separate view for missing evidence, even when work has been reported complete.

PNNL’s Small Building O&M Checklist is a planning reference for identifying maintenance opportunities and organizing them by system or frequency. Adapt it to the building. It is not a universal service schedule or a substitute for equipment instructions or applicable requirements.

Use these columns in a spreadsheet

The download has the following ten columns and one clearly fictional example row. Delete that example before using the template for your own records. Keep access codes and unnecessary personal information out of shared copies.

ColumnWhat to record
Asset or locationA stable equipment identifier or the location being maintained.
Recurring taskThe specific planned work; separate new defects into their own follow-up.
Assigned toThe person responsible for getting the task completed and checked.
Scheduled dueThe original deadline or due month, at the precision supported by the plan.
Actual service date or monthWhen work happened. Use YYYY-MM-DD for a known day or YYYY-MM when only the month is known.
Evidence receivedWhen the supporting report arrived; leave blank while pending.
Service report referenceA reference to the evidence in your approved records location.
Next dueThe next deadline once confirmed; leave blank when its basis is unresolved.
Next due sourceThe manual section, service plan, or qualified provider’s direction supporting that date and its starting point.
Follow-upOutstanding work, evidence, or confirmation, with an owner and follow-up date.

Import date-or-month columns as text if your spreadsheet would otherwise convert a month into an invented first-of-month date. Keep past service rows; add the next occurrence as a new row so the history remains visible.

Fictional example: A November service with a December report

A ventilation fan is due for service on November 30, 2026. The contractor performs the service on November 27, and the service report arrives on December 3.

  • Scheduled due: 2026-11-30.
  • Actual service date: 2026-11-27.
  • Evidence received: 2026-12-03.
  • Service report reference: Fictional FAN-01 report in the approved records location.
  • Next due: Not yet confirmed. Request the interval and its starting point from the qualified provider.

Count the service in November’s maintenance activity. Before the report arrives, label the entry “Service reported November 27; report pending.” When it arrives, check that it supports the work and date, then update the evidence status. Do not move the service into December just because you entered it or received the report then.

Keep the December evidence receipt visible so anyone reviewing an earlier month-end report can see what was known at that time. This is a maintenance activity record, not an accounting treatment or proof of payment.

Close the task without losing the next one

  1. Confirm the work. Record what was performed, the actual date or month, who confirmed it, and any unresolved issue. Keep “reported complete” separate from “checked against evidence.”
  2. Track missing proof. Request the service report and record an owner and follow-up date. Store the report in your approved records location; reference it from the calendar.
  3. Confirm the recurrence rule. Some schedules follow fixed calendar dates; others run from actual service or another defined event. Record which rule applies and where it came from. Ask the qualified provider when unclear.
  4. Create the next occurrence. Use the confirmed rule. Late data entry or late paperwork must not silently reset the next due date. If a correction changes a due date, record the reason rather than overwriting the history without explanation.

What if I only know the month?

Record “2026-11 — exact day unknown” in the actual service field, or keep “2026-11” with that explanation in Follow-up. Do not choose November 1 or November 30 to make a form accept the entry. If a day-specific recurrence depends on the missing day, leave the next date unconfirmed and follow up with the provider.

What should I review at month-end?

Review four lists: work due, work performed, evidence still missing, and the next confirmed services. Filter completed activity by actual service date or month, and evidence follow-up by its own status. A calendar check-off alone does not establish that the work was performed or that an invoice was paid.
